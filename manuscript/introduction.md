# Introduction

## Paragraph 1: The alveolar epithelium

Every breath depends on a thin, fragile layer of cells deep in the lung. The alveolar epithelium — composed of type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) cells — lines approximately 480 million air sacs where oxygen enters the bloodstream and carbon dioxide is released. AT1 cells are extraordinarily flat, spanning roughly 95% of the alveolar surface area to form the gas-exchange barrier. AT2 cells, smaller and cuboidal, perform two functions essential for lung homeostasis: they secrete surfactant, the lipid-protein film that prevents alveolar collapse, and they serve as the resident stem cells of the alveolar compartment. When AT1 cells are lost to injury, AT2 cells proliferate and differentiate to replace them through a well-characterized transitional state marked by KRT8, CLDN4, and SFN expression (Barkauskas et al., 2013; Kobayashi et al., 2020; Strunz et al., 2020). This AT2-to-AT1 differentiation axis is the lung's primary mechanism of alveolar self-repair.

## Paragraph 2: COVID-19 as extreme perturbation

In lethal COVID-19, this regenerative system is pushed beyond its capacity. SARS-CoV-2 enters AT2 cells through the ACE2 receptor, directly infecting the cells the lung depends on for both surfactant production and regeneration (Ziegler et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2020). The host immune response — particularly a dysregulated inflammatory cascade — inflicts massive collateral damage on the alveolar epithelium. AT1 cells are destroyed, eliminating gas-exchange surface. AT2 cells are simultaneously injured and called upon to repair the damage, creating a biological paradox: the progenitor cells tasked with rebuilding the epithelium are themselves under assault. Autopsy studies consistently describe diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), loss of normal alveolar architecture, accumulation of protein-rich edema, and the appearance of abnormal transitional cells that are neither fully AT2 nor fully AT1 — cells apparently caught mid-repair, unable to complete the differentiation program (Borczuk et al., 2020; Melms et al., 2021).

## Paragraph 3: Limitations of standard approaches

The most common strategy in single-cell genomics is to cluster cells into discrete types and compare gene expression between clusters or conditions. This approach has been applied productively to COVID-19 lung atlases, revealing changes in cell-type composition and identifying differentially expressed genes (Melms et al., 2021; Delorey et al., 2021). However, it has a fundamental limitation: it forces what may be a continuous biological process into discrete bins. If alveolar cells under COVID-19 injury are moving through a graded series of worsening states — from mildly stressed to actively inflamed to attempting repair to failing to undergoing programmed cell death — then clustering will either merge these states or split them at arbitrary boundaries. Differential expression between "healthy" and "COVID" reveals what changed on average but cannot resolve the order of molecular events, the existence of branching paths, or the position along the failure process where individual cells reside.

## Paragraph 4: The trajectory and manifold approach

A more informative approach treats each cell's gene expression profile as a position in a continuous biological landscape — the transcriptomic manifold. On this landscape, healthy cells should occupy a compact, coherent region reflecting stable homeostatic identity. COVID-19 cells, if the failure process is continuous, should spread outward from this region along paths associated with specific injury programs. By reconstructing these paths using trajectory inference and ordering cells along them with pseudotime, we can test whether the molecular evidence is consistent with a staged failure model — and characterize the stages at the level of coordinated gene programs. This approach is well-established in developmental biology for reconstructing differentiation trajectories from single-cell snapshots (Haghverdi et al., 2016; Setty et al., 2019) and has been applied to lung injury models (Strunz et al., 2020), though not to characterize the full arc of alveolar failure in lethal COVID-19 with systematic robustness validation.

## Paragraph 5: This study

Here, we analyze alveolar epithelial cells from the Columbia/NYP COVID-19 Lung Atlas (SCP1219), a publicly available single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset of 116,313 cells from healthy and lethal COVID-19 lungs (Melms et al., 2021). We construct a transcriptomic manifold of alveolar cell states, infer pseudotime trajectories rooted in healthy homeostasis, and score cells for eight curated gene programs spanning interferon response, inflammation, repair, and apoptosis. We frame the observed patterns as *epithelial robustness collapse* — the progressive loss of the system's ability to absorb perturbation and maintain function — and define this concept operationally through five convergent lines of evidence. To distinguish genuine biological structure from analytical artifacts, we perform ten systematic ablation analyses that stress-test every major methodological choice. The result is a rigorously validated, trajectory-centered map of how the alveolar epithelial system moves from function to failure under extreme perturbation.
